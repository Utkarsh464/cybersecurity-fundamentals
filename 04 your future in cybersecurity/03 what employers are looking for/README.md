## What I Learned

Having covered the market (Module 1) and the roles (Module 2), Module 3 is the practical question: **what exactly gets you hired?** The module breaks it into skill tiers and gives the honest picture of how employers actually screen.

**Baseline skills.** The floor everyone must have, whatever the role: networking fundamentals (TCP/IP, DNS, HTTP), operating systems (Linux + Windows), scripting/automation (Python/Bash), the CIA triad and risk vocabulary, and basic log/tool literacy. Without the baseline, nothing else matters — this is the "you need to be able to stand before you run" layer. All of it I already have from the three completed courses.

**Workplace skills.** The differentiators the module says are _most_ tested in interviews and _least_ teachable. Communication (translating risk to non-technical stakeholders and writing clear incident reports), problem-solving under pressure, adaptability, attention to detail, and **continuous learning** — the field rewrites itself yearly, so the hireable trait is the ability to keep absorbing. The IBM Cost of a Data Breach 2024 finding returns here: **employee training was the #1 factor reducing breach costs** — workplace skills are financially measurable, not just nice-to-have.

**Specialized skills.** The role-specific depth that takes the baseline and points it at a job. For SOC: SIEM (QRadar, Splunk), EDR, log analysis, threat hunting. For pen testing: exploitation frameworks (Metasploit), Burp Suite, vulnerability scanners, report writing. For GRC: NIST CSF / ISO 27001 / SOC 2, audit methodology, regulatory knowledge (HIPAA, PCI-DSS, GDPR). For cloud: AWS/Azure/GCP security anatomy, IAM, cloud-native architecture. The module emphasizes: **the baseline is shared, the specialization is the job.**

**What employers test.** The module is candid that employers screen for _demonstrable_ evidence — labs, CTF solutions, writeups, GitHub repos, a portfolio — because they cannot trust credentials alone. It also flags the reality that **certifications (Security+, etc.) are the filter that gets you past HR**, but the hands-on evidence is what gets you the offercars. The credential + this repo combo is exactly the "filter + evidence" pairing the module describes.

## What Stood Out to Me

The reframing of **"training as the #1 breach-cost reducer"** from a financial report about _organizations_ into an argument about _me_: if training is the single highest-ROI security investment organizations make, then a candidate who demonstrably self-trains (and documents it, with sources, as this repo does) is carrying the exact behavior that report rewards. My repo is not just study notes — it is the evidence of the single most valuable trait the industry says it is starved for.

The **baseline-is-shared, specialization-is-the-job** point also settles a question I've been circling: "should I learn everything?" No — you learn the baseline, then you _pick a lane_ (SOC, offense, GRC, cloud) and go deep. That permission to specialize is what the whole 4-course plan has been building toward.

## Key Takeaways

- Skills split into **baseline → workplace → specialized** — each tier gates the next.
- **Workplace skills (communication, problem-solving, adaptability, continuous learning) are what employers most test.**
- **Training is the #1 breach-cost reducer** — self-documentation is cargo evidence of the most-wanted trait.
- **Specialize after the baseline** — pick a lane (SOC / offense / GRC / cloud) and go deep.
- **Credentials get you past HR; demonstrable evidence gets you the offer.**
- The repo + credential is the **filter + evidence** pairing employers describe.
